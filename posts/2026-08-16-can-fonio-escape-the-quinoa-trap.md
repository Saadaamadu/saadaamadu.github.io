Quinoa used to be a subsistence crop grown by Andean farmers, largely invisible to the rest of the world. Then, in the early 2000s, it became a "superfood." Global demand exploded, prices for Peruvian and Bolivian farmers shot up, and for a while it looked like a genuine development win — a crop that had sustained Indigenous communities for thousands of years finally getting its due, with the people who'd always grown it finally getting paid for it.

Then production spread to over 120 countries, including large mechanized operations in the U.S. and China. Global supply surged, prices collapsed by nearly half between 2013 and 2015, and a lot of the long-term value settled somewhere else entirely — with processors and retailers turning quinoa into branded, ready-to-eat pouches, while the Andean farmers who'd pioneered the market were left competing on a price they no longer controlled.

I think about that story a lot, because fonio is starting to look like it's heading somewhere similar.

## An old grain, a new market

Fonio is one of West Africa's oldest cultivated cereals — domesticated more than five thousand years ago, drought-tolerant, fast-maturing, able to grow on marginal soil that a lot of other crops can't touch. In Northern Ghana, where I do my research, it's exactly the kind of crop you'd want more of as the region deals with worsening water stress and degraded soils. And yet it's historically stayed a subsistence crop, grown at small scale and eaten locally, mostly because processing it is brutal: getting the husk off traditionally takes multiple rounds of hand-pounding, which makes it slow and labor-intensive to produce at any real scale.

That's now changing. International development money is flowing in — USAID invested in a fonio processing facility in Tamale in 2024, aiming to scale local processing capacity more than threefold. Development organizations and agribusiness firms have started marketing fonio as a "supergrain" for gluten-free, health-conscious consumers abroad. On paper, that's a good thing: more demand, more investment, more reason for farmers to keep growing an indigenous, climate-resilient crop instead of switching to maize.

But I keep coming back to the same question: who actually captures the value when that demand shows up?

## The quinoa trap

Quinoa is the cautionary tale, but teff — Ethiopia's staple grain — might be the more instructive one. Like fonio, teff needs intensive processing before it's usable, which limited its export potential for a long time. When foreign firms developed mechanized processing methods, some of them tried to patent those methods — effectively attempting to claim ownership over how an indigenous Ethiopian grain gets turned into food. Ethiopia pushed back by restricting raw teff exports to protect its own supply and keep processing (and the value that comes with it) inside the country. That protected them, but it also capped how much Ethiopian producers could benefit from the very demand that made teff valuable in the first place.

That's the trap, basically: a crop nobody wanted becomes a crop everybody wants, and the region that grows it ends up supplying raw material while the profitable part — processing, packaging, branding, distribution — happens somewhere else, owned by somebody else.

For fonio, the mechanics of that risk are already visible. Dehusking and polishing the grain at scale is technically hard, which means whoever solves that problem well, and cheaply, ends up holding a lot of leverage over everyone downstream of them. If that "whoever" is a foreign firm with a patent, Northern Ghana could end up in the same position Peru did with quinoa: present at the birth of a boom, absent from most of its profits.

## What actually changes the outcome

None of this is inevitable, and the more I dig into the comparative cases, the more it looks like the outcome comes down to a few concrete, unglamorous things:

- **Who owns the processing infrastructure.** Community-owned or cooperative mills change everything about where value ends up. In Bolivia, quinoa cooperatives like ANAPQUI helped farmers secure fair-trade certification, coordinate exports, and negotiate as a bloc instead of individually — which is a large part of why some Bolivian producers fared better than their Peruvian counterparts.
- **Land tenure clarity, especially for women.** Much of Northern Ghana operates under customary land systems where chiefs and family lineages control land allocation, often without formal documentation. That's manageable when land isn't worth much. It becomes a serious risk the moment a crop grown on it becomes commercially valuable — women farmers in particular can lose access once that happens. Ethiopia's joint land certification program, which registered land in both spouses' names, is one existing model for protecting against that.
- **Reserving some of the market for home.** Export demand is great until it isn't — prices are volatile, and a region that bets everything on foreign buyers is exposed to swings it can't control. School-feeding programs and public procurement that guarantee some domestic demand (Peru does this with quinoa and native potatoes; Brazil requires a share of school food purchases to come from local family farmers) give producers a floor that doesn't depend on what happens in export markets.
- **Keeping the technology open.** If dehulling and polishing technology develops through public research and university extension systems rather than proprietary patents, local firms actually have a shot at building and owning that infrastructure themselves, instead of licensing it from someone who got there first.

None of this is about whether fonio *should* be commercialized — it's happening regardless. The question I keep sitting with is whether it happens in a way that's structured, from the start, to keep the value in the communities that have been growing this crop for five thousand years. Quinoa's story suggests that's not the default outcome. It has to be built in on purpose.
